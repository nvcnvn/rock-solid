# LSM runs spike

**Question.** How many sorted runs does size-tiered compaction of in-memory runs leave an index with, what does a lookup cost over them without Bloom filters, and does bucketing every run under 1 MiB together keep the chunk index's tiny runs few enough? And do range removals, as RocksDB's `DeleteRange`, or eager merges of runs full of removals, bring a chunk rewritten whole after many small extents back to one search per lookup ([open questions](../../docs/design.md#roadmap-and-open-questions))?

**Setup.** Linux 6.18.44 in a Firecracker VM, 4 vCPUs of an Intel Xeon at 2.1 GHz, Go 1.27.0. **The machine was shared with six other agents running benchmarks**, so the harness reports thread CPU time per operation (`CLOCK_THREAD_CPUTIME_ID` on a locked thread) and counts, not wall-clock latency; even so, absolute lookup costs here are several times an idle laptop's (the repository's own `BenchmarkGet` took 2.7 to 3.3 µs per get here). Written against rock-solid commit `dbae461` (`internal/lsm` and `internal/chunkstore` as of `676095b`).

- The harness ([main.go](main.go), [env.go](env.go)) drives the real `internal/lsm` and `internal/chunkstore` through the module's `replace` directive: records go in as `disklog.Event`s, a checkpoint writes every piece `NextPiece` asks for, and after each flush every compaction `PickCompaction` offers runs to completion, synchronously.
- [lsmx/](lsmx/) is a copy of `internal/lsm` with the candidate fixes behind `Config` switches; [cix/](cix/) a copy of `internal/chunkstore/index.go` over it:
  - **cursor**: a floor keeps each source's candidate between steps, so stepping past a removal searches only the sources holding its key.
  - **range**: `Tree.DeleteRange(lo, hi)`. A memtable keeps a list of fragments; removing a range turns the memtable's own entries in it into removals, and a later put cuts its key out of the fragment, so a memtable's fragments hide its own entries too. A run keeps its fragments beside its blocks (in memory only; the spike does not encode them into pieces) and its own entries win over them. A get stops at the first source holding the key or a fragment over it; a floor moves every source older than a covering fragment below the fragment's start in one search; a seek moves them past its end. A flush or merge keeps a fragment only while an older run holds a key in it and drops the removals its kept fragments cover. `cix.Index.RangeTrim = 2` makes a write that removes two or more extents' keys remove them with one range removal.
  - **density**: a run whose removals are at least half its entries (and at least 256) merges with every older run down to the oldest holding a key it removes, as RocksDB's deletion-triggered and Pebble's tombstone-density compactions do.
- Correctness of the copies: `lsmx` reads as a map under 3,000 random puts, removals, range removals, flushes and compactions for 60 seeds in five configurations, including real size tiering (`SmallRun: 1`); a planted bug (merge output losing its fragments) fails 119 of them. `cix` with range trims matches the real chunk index block for block, and passes its `Check`, under random writes, punches, freezes, whole rewrites, flushes and compactions; a planted bug (puts not cut out of memtable fragments) fails 13.
- Workloads. **A3**: 500,000 random 16-byte keys split evenly into 1 to 32 runs. **A1**: the MetaStore memory spike's file model (an attribute record and a directory entry per file), about a million files, a flush every 2,000, 20,000 or 200,000 creates, each flush also overwriting 20% as many attributes and removing 2% as many files. **A2**: a 512 GiB disk filled with whole chunks, 16 chunks (64 MiB, the checkpoint interval) per flush, each chunk four 1 MiB records, then the same with 164 random 4 KiB overwrites (1% of the bytes) per flush. **B**: 20,000 chunks written whole and a chunk of 1,024 one-block extents, then that chunk rewritten whole in one 1,024-block record or four 256-block records; lookups of every block of the chunk while the rewrite is in the memtable, after its flush, and after up to 3,000 more flushes of 16 new whole chunks each. **C**: a block volume of 512 chunks, 100 flushes, each after 4,096 random 4 KiB writes and 12 whole-chunk rewrites.

```
./run.sh     # about 25 minutes on the shared VM; tests, then results-a.txt, results-b.txt, results-c.txt
```

Expected before the run: runs settle at about three per size tier; lookups cost one search per run; the tiny-run bucket keeps the chunk index under four small runs; range removals bring the rewritten chunk to a couple of searches; density merges fix it only once the removals are flushed, at the cost of rewriting the run holding the old keys.

## Conclusions

1. **Size-tiered runs settle at about three per size tier**, four times apart from 1 MiB: at most 3·log4(index ÷ 1 MiB) + 3 runs (10 at 30 MB, 13 at 141 MB measured; about 24 for a 16 GiB shard). Smaller flushes mean more runs: a MetaStore index flushed every 2,000 creates averaged 7.3 runs, every 200,000 at most 3.
2. **Bucketing every run under 1 MiB together keeps the tiny runs few**: never more than 3 under 1 MiB, since the fourth merges them. It costs rewriting the chunk index's small runs about 47 times, which is 0.26% of the log's bytes under whole-chunk writes, so the rule stays.
3. **Without Bloom filters a lookup costs one search per run**, about 0.3 µs per run for a hit and 0.6 µs for a miss or a floor on this machine, with no allocation. A miss in a 16 GiB shard's two dozen runs would cost on the order of 15 µs here; whether the MetaStore needs Bloom filters or a run cap before the block frontend is a new open question.
4. **A removal of a key an older run holds can outlive a disk's worth of writes.** Size tiering merges the removal with the old key only when the newer runs have grown to the old run's size: 3,000 flushes (192 GiB) were not enough for a 4.6 MB run, and lookups of the rewritten chunk stayed at 200 to 730 µs, writes into it at 140 to 380 µs.
5. **Range removals fix it; merges triggered by removals do not.** A write that removes several extents' keys removes them with one range removal: lookups take 2 searches (0.4 to 1.8 µs) from the moment the write is applied, nothing has to be merged, and the mixed block-volume workload's lookups drop from 12.4 to 5.2 µs. A density-triggered merge leaves the memtable phase (up to `max_replay_bytes` of records) at 85 to 230 µs per lookup and rewrites whole the run holding the old keys, which can be the index's largest. Keeping each source's floor candidate alone changes nothing for this case, since both sources hold every key stepped over, but it is how range removals jump, and it cut the mixed workload's lookups by 30%.
6. A memtable's range removal must hide the memtable's own older entries without leaving them to step over: it turns them into removals, and a later put cuts its key out of the fragment. Runs keep their fragments beside their blocks, and a run's own entries win over its fragments, which a merge needs since it unites its inputs' fragments.
